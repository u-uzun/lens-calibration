    User manual

    1. What this tool is

    This is a lens distortion corrector for cameras you cannot reach. In factories and warehouses, CCTV cameras are usually mounted high up and were installed years ago. Walking a checkerboard in front of them, at the right distances and angles, is often impractical and sometimes impossible.

    Instead of a checkerboard, this tool uses objects that are already in the scene and that you know to be straight in the real world: a column, a building edge, a floor joint, a shelf edge, a crane rail. You mark them, and the tool searches for the radial distortion correction that makes them as straight as possible.

    The method is not new. In the literature it is called plumb-line calibration and it goes back to the 1970s. What this tool adds is a workflow you can run on a single frame in a browser, plus several checks that tell you when the answer should not be trusted.

    Everything runs locally. The frame you load never leaves your computer; there is no server and nothing is uploaded.

    2. What it does not do

    It does not give you the camera matrix. No focal length, no principal point. The reason is mathematical, not a missing feature: a straight line stays straight under any focal length, so a criterion built on straightness carries no information about focal length at all. However many objects you mark, it will not appear.

    It does not reproduce the lens's true coefficients. Straightness is a weak constraint on the shape of the radial profile: two noticeably different corrections can straighten the same objects to within a fraction of a pixel while moving a pixel near the frame edge tens of pixels differently. So the coefficients here do not match a checkerboard calibration of the same camera, and should not be expected to.

    What I did verify is the thing that actually matters. On my own camera I ran both methods side by side - a 54-frame checkerboard calibration through OpenCV, and this tool on two different scenes - and compared them over the region the checkerboard genuinely covered. Once a ground-plane homography was fitted on the corrected image, the two agreed to within roughly one to four centimetres on the ground depending on the scene, for a camera about 6 m up covering some 10 m of width.

    What that means in practice. It is enough for zone intrusion, proximity and speed work, where tolerances are in metres. It is not enough for camera pose or 3D measurement; those still need a checkerboard.

    3. Quick start

    1. Press Sample frame to see the tool working on a synthetic image, or Load frame to use your own.
    2. Click 4 to 10 points roughly along an object you know is straight. Press Enter (or Finish object).
    3. Repeat for at least three objects. Set one of them to check in the list.
    4. Press Solve coefficients.
    5. Drag the Transition slider from Raw to Corrected, or press Play transition, and look at the result.
    6. Read the verdict box and any orange warning. Run Blind test and Uncertainty.
    7. Copy k1 and k2 from the results, or save the full log.

    4. The sample frame

    The sample frame is a synthetic image generated in the browser. Straight lines are drawn, then bent by a distortion with coefficients the tool picks and knows exactly. Those true values are printed at the bottom of the image.

    It exists so you can check the tool against a known answer before trusting it on your own data. After solving, the verdict line reports the true k1 next to the recovered one. If they are close, the measurement chain is working on your machine, in your browser.

    Use it to learn the workflow, and come back to it whenever you suspect something is wrong.

    5. Loading your own frame

    Press Load frame and pick any still image: a screenshot from your video management system, a frame grabbed from an RTSP stream, a photo. The frame is processed at its own resolution; it is only scaled on screen to fit the window.

    A few things matter in the frame you choose:

    • No digital correction in the camera. If your camera has a lens distortion correction (LDC) setting and it is on, the frame is already partly corrected and the result will describe whatever is left, not the lens.
    • Sharp and well lit. You will be clicking on edges; motion blur and noise cost you accuracy.
    • No cropping or resizing after capture. Cropping moves the optical centre away from the middle of the frame and the model assumes it is in the middle. Uniform scaling is harmless.
    • The same camera settings you will use in production. On a varifocal lens, changing zoom or focus changes the distortion.

    If you have marked objects on your own frame and then press Load frame or Sample frame, the tool asks for confirmation first, because your work would be lost.

    6. Marking objects - the part that decides your result

    This is where almost all the quality of the answer comes from. Everything else is arithmetic.

    What makes a good object

    The tool is trying to work out how bending grows as you move from the centre of the frame towards the corners. It can only see that growth where you give it data. So the single most valuable object you can mark is:

    One long line that passes near the centre of the frame and runs out to both edges.

    Such a line crosses a wide range of distances from the centre all by itself, which is what pins down the shape of the curve. One object like that is worth more than ten short ones.

    Lines that carry no information

    A line that points straight at the centre of the frame carries no information at all, however long it is. Radial distortion pushes pixels outwards from the centre, so it stretches such a line along its own direction without bending it. The tool detects these and leaves them out of the solve; they appear greyed out in the list.

    Before marking something, picture an arrow from the centre of the frame to that object. If the object is perpendicular to the arrow, it is useful. If it is parallel to the arrow, it is not.

    A line passing exactly through the centre is the worst case. A line passing near the centre, offset a little, and running to both edges is the best case.

    Things that are not actually straight

    Choose objects whose straightness you would be willing to defend:

    • Rope, string and cable sag under their own weight. Power lines are curves, not lines. Never use them.
    • Plastered cornices, rendered wall junctions and old timber can be a few pixels off straight over a long span, which is the same size as the signal being measured.
    • Rails and channels in a floor may genuinely curve. Follow them with your eye before marking.
    • Lens-distorted edges you have already corrected elsewhere in a pipeline are not raw data.

    If you need to create a straight object on purpose: a strip of tape stuck to the floor, a chalk line snapped on a slab, or a long aluminium profile all work well and none of them sag. (Still easier than asking the facility for a forklift, a man-up or a ladder so you can climb up and wave a checkerboard around.)

    How to click

    Put 4 to 10 points along the object, spread over its whole length rather than bunched at one end. The points do not need to be evenly spaced. Zoom in with the mouse wheel where the edge is thin; once you are zoomed in, pan with Shift + drag or the middle mouse button. Press Enter when done.

    You can drag a point afterwards to adjust it. Undo point removes the last point of the object you are drawing. del in the list removes a whole object.

    7. Object roles: fit and check

    Every object in the list is either in the fit or used as a check.

    • fit - the coefficients are derived from this object.
    • check - this object is left out of the solve entirely. Afterwards the tool measures how straight it became. Because the solver never saw it, this is a real test and not a self-assessment.

    Always keep at least one check object, and choose one that is visibly bent to begin with. A check object that was almost straight already proves nothing.

    Mark at least three objects. Beyond that, more is not automatically better: it depends on how well chosen they are and on what the scene actually offers. Three well-placed objects beat ten poor ones. With three, use two for the fit and one as a check; with eight, two or three checks are reasonable.

    8. Solving

    Solve coefficients

    Runs the search. The tool looks for the correction that minimises the average bending of all the objects in the fit, measured as the perpendicular spread of the points around the straight line fitted through them, divided by the object's length so that long and short objects count equally.

    k1 and k2

    k1 alone describes a simple barrel or pincushion. k2 adds a second term that lets the curve change shape towards the edges.

    k2 is off by default and that is deliberate. Hand-clicked points carry a position error of a few pixels. On a weakly distorted frame the curvature being measured is also a few pixels, so the signal sits inside the noise. Solving both coefficients at once lets k2 absorb that noise and settle on a large value with the opposite sign to k1, producing a profile that rises and then falls with radius. No real lens does that.

    After a solve, the tool checks whether a second term would genuinely help and offers it with a one-click button if so. It looks at two things: whether the flatness actually improves, and whether the per-object solutions vary systematically with distance from the centre, which would be the physical reason for needing a second term. If the first is true but the second is not, treat the recommendation with caution.

    Why the optical centre is fixed at the middle of the frame

    The optical centre is where the lens axis actually meets the sensor. It is almost never the exact middle of the frame. On a typical camera it sits a few tens of pixels off, which corresponds to only a few tens of micrometres of mechanical eccentricity in the lens mount - an entirely ordinary assembly tolerance.

    This tool assumes it is the middle anyway, and that is a deliberate choice. The only evidence for the true centre in straight-line data is a small asymmetry between how much objects bend on opposite sides of the frame. Measured on a real camera that asymmetry is around six to eight percent, which is far too weak to recover the centre reliably. An earlier version of this tool let the solver search for it; the result barely improved while the extra freedom gave the solver somewhere to hide error. The option was removed.

    The cost of the assumption is real but bounded: it is the main reason the coefficients here differ from a checkerboard calibration. It is also largely absorbed when you fit a homography on the corrected image, which is why the end-to-end error stays at the centimetre level.

    k1 manually

    Type a value and press apply to see what any coefficient does, without solving. Useful for building intuition and for reproducing someone else's number.

    9. The view controls

    • Transition - fades between the raw frame and the corrected one. The two buttons jump straight to either end. Play transition animates it, which is the easiest way to see what changed.
    • Framing (alpha) - correcting an image pushes pixels outwards, so either the corners spill outside the frame or gaps appear at the edges. This slider chooses the compromise. Neutral scale keeps the scale at the centre unchanged, All pixels keeps everything at the cost of empty corners, No gaps crops until no gap is left.
    • Exaggeration - multiplies the correction for display only. It does not change the solution. Use it to see the shape of a weak correction.
    • Arrows - after a solve, each arrow shows where one pixel is moved to: tail at the raw position, head at the corrected one. They are short near the centre and long towards the corners, which is the visual signature of radial distortion. They change with the solution and with the two sliders above.
    • Border - draws where the edge of the raw frame ends up after correction.
    • Grid - a plain reference grid over the image.
    • Mouse wheel zooms, Shift + drag or the middle button pans once you are zoomed in, fit at the bottom right returns to the whole frame.

    Hovering a row in the object list highlights that object on the frame and fades the others. The number badge on the frame matches the number in the list.

    10. Reading the results

    • k1, k2 - the coefficients, in the correction direction (see section 14).
    • cx, cy - the optical centre used. Always the middle of the frame; see section 8 for why.
    • Deviation from straight - the average bending of the objects in the fit, before and after. This is the number the solver minimised, so it will always improve. It is a progress indicator, not evidence.
    • Check object - the same measurement on the object the solver never saw. This is the number that matters. A large improvement here means the correction generalises beyond the data it was fitted to.
    • Corner displacement - how far the extreme corner of the frame moves. Gives you a feel for the strength of the correction.
    • Net shape change - the corner movement after removing the part that is just a uniform zoom. A pure zoom does not fix distortion, so this is the part that actually did something.
    • The profile plot - magnification against radius. It should rise smoothly from left to right. If it peaks and falls, the solution is not physical.

    11. Warnings and what they mean

    • "The selected objects may be too weak" (orange box) - the marked objects do not span enough of the range of distances from the centre. The box shows which radius bands are covered and how far each object reaches by itself. Fix it by adding one long object from near the centre out to an edge, not by adding more short ones.
    • "The solution hit the upper bound" - the search ran to its limit. Almost always caused by an object that carries no real information letting the solver run free. Look for a greyed-out object or one that points at the centre.
    • "The correction made the objects more bent" - the result is worse than doing nothing. Do not use it.
    • "The profile peaks and then falls" - the magnification curve is not monotonic, which no lens produces. Turn k2 off.
    • "The check object got worse" - the correction works on the objects it was fitted to but not elsewhere in the frame. Usually means the objects are clustered in one region.
    • "No check object" - you have no independent test. Set one object to check.
    • "Fewer than 2 objects in the fit" - the whole result rests on a single reference.
    • "carried too little information and were left out" - these objects point at the centre or are too short to bend measurably. Not an error; the tool is protecting the solve.

    12. The checks, and what each one can and cannot tell you

    Check object (holdout)

    An object left out of the solve, measured afterwards. Tests whether the correction generalises. The strongest single check in the tool.

    Blind test

    The tool adds a known amount of extra distortion to your frame, moves your marked points with it, and solves everything again from scratch. If the measurement chain works, the recovered coefficients should match the predicted ones. The criterion is the difference between the two correction curves in pixels, not the coefficients themselves, because k1 and k2 can substitute for each other.

    Your frame and points are restored automatically afterwards, so you can run it as often as you like.

    Uncertainty and leave-one-out

    Uncertainty re-runs the solve hundreds of times with marking noise added, and reports how much the coefficients move. Leave-one-out removes each object in turn and reports the result each time. If one object moves the answer by more than twice the uncertainty, the tool says so by name; that object is carrying the result on its own and you should add another of similar length.

    Check whether the objects agree

    Solves each object on its own and lists what each one wants. If one object demands a very different coefficient from the rest, it is usually not actually straight. This is how you find a curved rail you mistook for a line.

    What none of them can tell you

    All of these measure self-consistency. A systematic error that affects every object in the same way will pass all of them. They can tell you when to distrust a result; they cannot promise that a result is correct. The only external check is a calibration from a different method on the same camera.

    13. Using the result in your pipeline

    Copy k1 and k2 from the results panel, or press Save & Download solution for a JSON file that also contains every marked point, so the solution can be recomputed independently. (A ready script that reads that downloaded JSON file and applies it to an RTSP stream is given below.)

    The correction is a fixed mapping for a given camera, so you build the remap once and apply it to every frame. In Python with OpenCV:

    import json, cv2, numpy as np

SOLUTION = "solution_log.json"        # the file this tool downloaded
STREAM   = "rtsp://user:pass@192.168.1.64:554/Streaming/Channels/101"

def load_solution(path):
    with open(path, encoding="utf-8") as f:
        s = json.load(f)["solution"]
    return s["k1"], s["k2"], s.get("framing_zoom", 1.0)

def build_maps(w, h, k1, k2, zoom=1.0):
    R = np.hypot(w, h) / 2.0          # same normalisation as the tool
    cx, cy = w / 2.0, h / 2.0
    yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:h, 0:w].astype(np.float32)
    dx, dy = (xx - cx) / zoom, (yy - cy) / zoom
    rc = np.hypot(dx, dy) / R         # radius wanted in the corrected image
    rr = rc.copy()
    for _ in range(20):               # invert rr*(1+k1*rr^2+k2*rr^4) = rc
        f  = rr * (1 + k1*rr**2 + k2*rr**4) - rc
        df = 1 + 3*k1*rr**2 + 5*k2*rr**4
        rr -= f / np.maximum(df, 1e-6)
    s = np.where(rc > 1e-9, rr / np.maximum(rc, 1e-9), 1.0)
    return (cx + dx*s).astype(np.float32), (cy + dy*s).astype(np.float32)

k1, k2, zoom = load_solution(SOLUTION)
cap = cv2.VideoCapture(STREAM)
ok, frame = cap.read()
h, w = frame.shape[:2]
mx, my = build_maps(w, h, k1, k2, zoom)    # built once, reused every frame

while ok:
    fixed = cv2.remap(frame, mx, my, cv2.INTER_LINEAR)
    # run your detection / tracking / homography on "fixed"
    ok, frame = cap.read()

    The maps are built once and reused, so the per-frame cost is a single remap, which is cheap enough for real time even on an edge device.

    If you then need ground coordinates, fit your homography on the corrected image, using four or more points whose positions on the ground you know. Fitting it on the corrected image is what makes the method work: the homography absorbs the part of the residual error that behaves like a projective transform.

    14. The model

    r_corrected = r_raw * (1 + k1*r^2 + k2*r^4), where r = radius from the optical centre divided by half the image diagonal.

    Note the direction. These coefficients describe correction: given a point you observe, where should it go. OpenCV's coefficients describe distortion: given an ideal point, where does the lens put it. Both describe the same lens from opposite ends.

    The directions are not related by a sign flip. Converting between them means sampling one map and refitting the other. Converting to OpenCV's format also needs a focal length, because OpenCV divides the radius by the focal length while this tool divides it by half the diagonal, and this tool does not produce a focal length.

    So do not paste k1 and k2 from here into cv2.undistort. Use the code in section 13 instead.

    15. Honest limitations

    • No camera matrix, no focal length, no principal point.
    • No tangential distortion. The model is purely radial and assumes the optical centre is the middle of the frame unless you ask otherwise.
    • The coefficients will not match a checkerboard calibration of the same camera, for the reasons in section 2.
    • Accuracy depends on the scene. A frame with long, well-spread straight objects gives a much better result than one without, and no amount of care in clicking makes up for a scene that has nothing useful in it.
    • The checks detect inconsistent data. They do not detect a consistent but wrong answer.
    • This is a working tool for a specific situation, not a replacement for proper calibration and not a commercial product.
